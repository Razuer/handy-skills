A working definition

Calm software, in one breath.

Definition

Calm software

attention · ambient · invariant under interruption

A system designed so that a user's attention is the scarce resource being optimised against — not engagement, not throughput, not retention. It gets out of the way, then earns the right to come back.

Three commitments
  • i.
    Defaults respect the user. Quiet on arrival. No setup ceremony, no first-run modals, no notification permission on day one.
  • ii.
    The periphery does the work. Most state lives in ambient surfaces — menu bars, widgets, glanceable views — not centre-stage windows.
  • iii.
    Interruption is a last resort. A notification must clear a bar three times higher than the current default in your toolkit.

01 — Why now

Three signals it's overdue.

None of these are new on their own — the change is that they're now all true at the same time.

  • i.
    The notification budget is spent. Median knowledge worker sees 217 push notifications per weekday across personal + work devices. Engagement on each has fallen 38% YoY since 2023.
  • ii.
    Local inference is finally cheap enough. A 4B-parameter model fits on consumer silicon. The "infer locally, sync silently" pattern is no longer a research prototype.
  • iii.
    Regulators are writing it down. The EU's Digital Fairness Act draft (Apr 2026) explicitly names "attention-extractive defaults" as enforceable. Compliance becomes a product surface, not a footer link.

Five steps, end to end.

Attention budget

Estimate cost in user-seconds before any code is written. Numbers, not vibes.

spreadsheet · day 0

Ambient first

Sketch the peripheral surface — widget, menubar, glance view — before a full window.

paper · day 1–2

Build quiet

Default state: no badges, no sounds, no first-run modal. Earn each interruption.

code · week 1–3

field · week 4

A binary call. We do not park features. The graveyard is public and linked in the docs.

03 — Method

Engagement design vs. attention design.

A · Conventional

Optimise for time spent in the app.

Every screen is a destination. Every notification is a re-entry hook. Success metric is daily active use.

  • Easy to A/B test against.
  • Aligned with ad-supported revenue.
  • Tends to degrade trust over 18+ months.
B · Calm

Optimise for task completion per minute of presence.

The app is a tool, not a destination. Re-entry is initiated by the user, not the system. Metric is time-to-done.

  • Harder to measure on quarterly cycles.
  • Compounds: retained users are the strongest signal.
  • Requires alignment from leadership, not just design.

03 — Method

Three attention modes, three surface types.

A · Background

Information you don't read until you need it.

Battery indicator. Sync status. Build state. The user notices only when something changes.

  • Glanceable in under 200ms.
  • State changes must be ignorable.
  • No interruption budget.
B · Periphery

Information you check, but don't dwell on.

A widget. A menubar dropdown. A glance view on the lock screen. The middle ground that most products skip.

  • Sub-five-second sessions.
  • One primary action, no nesting.
  • Where most of our work lives.
C · Centre

Information you give your full attention.

A document, a video call, an editor. Earned, never assumed. We design to leave it cleanly.

  • Always has a single exit.
  • No re-entry hooks on close.
  • Hardest to design well.

03 — Method

The attention cost of a feature.

A back-of-envelope model we run during step one. It's deliberately crude — being approximately right beats being precisely wrong.

Cf = Σu   pu · tu · (1 − du)

total attention cost of feature f, summed over the user base

Cf
Aggregate cost in user-seconds per week. Negative budgets kill the feature.
pu
Probability the feature surfaces to user u in a given week.
tu
Median time-to-dismiss when it does surface — measured in shipped prototypes.
du
Probability of desired engagement: user genuinely wanted this. From qualitative interviews, not telemetry.

03 — Method

The interruption gate, in code.

calm/gate.pystep 03 · build quiet
1# Every notification flows through here.
2# If it cannot defend itself, it does not fire.
3 
4def should_interrupt(event, user, ctx):
5cost = ctx.estimate_cost(event, user)
6value = event.expected_value(user)
7 
8if user.in_flow_state():
9return value > cost * 3.0 # 3× bar
10if ctx.is_off_hours(user):
11return value > cost * 5.0 # 5× bar
12 
13return value > cost

The default

If value doesn't beat cost, the event silently goes to the periphery instead. It is never lost — it's just not loud.

Flow state

Detected from focus-app windows, calendar, and keyboard cadence. We over-index on false positives here on purpose.

Off hours

Defined per user, not per timezone. The 5× bar is what kept this team off pager rotation through 2025.

05 — Takeaways

Three things to take home.

  1. i.

    The cost model matters more than the policy.

    Once you can put a number on what an interruption costs, the policy is downstream. Most teams skip straight to policy and argue forever.

    Section 03

  2. ii.

    The periphery is underbuilt almost everywhere.

    Most product teams design centre-stage screens and lock screens; the middle layer is left to platform defaults. That's where the wins live.

    Section 03

  3. iii.

    A public graveyard beats a private backlog.

    Shipping a list of killed features changed how the team made calls upstream. Hard to measure, but everyone who has done this says the same thing.

    Section 04

05 — What I got wrong

Three calls I'd undo if I could.

Each one is a real bug in the model above — not nice-to-haves.

  • i.

    We measured cost; we never measured regret.

    A missed notification has a cost too. Our model only captured one side of the ledger for the first 18 months.

    Fix shippedv4.1 adds a counterfactual "what if we had fired this" path.
  • ii.

    "Off hours" was tuned per user, not per role.

    On-call engineers wanted the loud defaults back at 3am. We learned this the hard way.

    Fix in v5Role-aware overrides land in Q3 2026; opt-in per team.
  • iii.

    We over-trusted self-reported trust scores.

    Users who liked the defaults rated them high; users who quietly turned them off rated them low and then left. We didn't separate the cohorts for nine months.

    OpenCurrently rebuilding the survey from scratch with an external team.
